## PART A: THEORY

### A1. What happens when two drugs are given together? (slide: Results of Combination Therapy)

| Result | Slide definition | Simple maths | Easy example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Additive** ("indifferent") | Combined activity **equals** the sum of the separate activities | 1 + 1 = 2 | Two painkillers with similar action |
| **Synergistic** | Combined activity is **greater** than the sum | 1 + 1 = 3 | Trimethoprim + sulfamethoxazole; amoxicillin + clavulanic acid; ampicillin + gentamicin |
| **Antagonistic** | Combined activity is **less** than the sum | 1 + 1 = 1 or less | Penicillin (needs growing bacteria) + tetracycline (stops growth); naloxone + morphine |

> **Note-check:** Strictly, "indifferent" (the combination is no better than the best single drug) is slightly different from "additive". The slide merges them. For exams use **additive = equals the sum**.

**Rationale:** Think of two people pushing a car. Additive = both push, the car moves twice as far. Synergy = they push **together in rhythm** and it moves far more. Antagonism = one pushes while the other pulls back.

### A2. When is it right to combine antibiotics? (slide: Indications)

1. **Prevention of resistant organisms.** Example: **TB** is always treated with 3 to 4 drugs. Bacteria that resist one drug are killed by the others.
2. **Polymicrobial infection** (many germs at once, e.g., intra-abdominal sepsis, diabetic foot). One drug cannot cover them all.
3. **Initial (empirical) therapy.** In a very sick patient, cover the likely germs before the lab result.
4. **Decreased toxicity.** A smaller dose of each drug lowers the side effects.
5. **Synergism.** The drugs together kill better than either alone.

> **Rule of thumb for nurses:** Combinations also have downsides: more side effects, more cost, more interactions and antagonism. They are used for a reason, not by habit.

### A3. Synergism example list (slide: Synergism)

**Key mechanism:** Beta-lactams **damage the cell wall**. This lets **aminoglycosides** (which normally cross the wall poorly) **enter the bacterium in higher amounts** and reach the ribosome. Result: **enhanced uptake of aminoglycosides**.

| Infection | Combination (from slide) |
|---|---|
| Enterococcal endocarditis | **Ampicillin + gentamicin** |
| Viridans streptococcal endocarditis | **Penicillin + gentamicin** |
| Staphylococcal bacteraemia | **Vancomycin + gentamicin** |
| Pseudomonas infections | **Beta-lactam + aminoglycoside** |

**Kenya link:** The standard treatment for **severe pneumonia in children** (MoH Basic Paediatric Protocols) is **benzylpenicillin (or ampicillin) + gentamicin**, a classic beta-lactam + aminoglycoside pair. Do not give them in the same syringe or IV line (they inactivate each other physically). Give separately.

### A4. Beta-lactamase: the enzyme that causes resistance (from past papers)

- **Beta-lactam antibiotics** (penicillins, cephalosporins, carbapenems) all contain a **beta-lactam ring**. That ring does the killing.
- **Beta-lactamase (penicillinase)** is an enzyme made by some bacteria (*Staph. aureus*, *E. coli*, *H. influenzae*) that **breaks the ring**, so the drug becomes inactive.
- **Two ways to beat it:**
  1. Add a **beta-lactamase inhibitor** (clavulanic acid, sulbactam, tazobactam). Example: **co-amoxiclav (Augmentin)** = amoxicillin + clavulanic acid. The clavulanic acid has almost **no antibacterial effect itself**; it only **protects** the amoxicillin. That is why the effect is called **synergistic** (clavulanic acid alone would do nothing, but together the result is far greater).
  2. Use a **beta-lactamase-resistant** drug: **flucloxacillin/cloxacillin** (penicillinase-resistant penicillins), later-generation cephalosporins, **carbapenems**.
- **Beta-lactamase does NOT destroy** sulfonamides, macrolides or tetracyclines. They use other resistance tricks (pumps out, altered targets).

### A5. Resistance and stewardship (extra, tested indirectly)
- **Resistance** appears when bacteria survive a drug and multiply.
- **Causes:** not completing courses, using antibiotics for viral illness, wrong doses, overuse of broad-spectrum drugs.
- **Stewardship rules for nurses:** give doses **on time**, **complete the course**, take cultures **before** the first dose, and keep **reserve drugs** (carbapenems, vancomycin) for resistant hospital infections.

## PART D: ANSWERS AND EXPLANATIONS

**Q1 → B.** Synergism = effect **greater than the sum**.
- A "double" is too specific (synergy is *more* than sum, not necessarily twice). C is **additive**. D is **antagonism**.
- *Tip:* If one option says "greater than the sum" and another says "double", choose "greater than the sum".

**Q2 → B. Additivity.** Equal to the sum (1 + 1 = 2).
- A = greater than sum. D = less. C "agonism" is about **receptors**, not drug combinations.

**Q3 → A. Synergism.**
- The stem has two clues: **different mechanisms** and **greater effect**. "Addictive effect" is a typo of **additive** (equal to the sum, not greater). "Interference" = one drug changes absorption/metabolism of another. "Displacement" = one drug pushes another off plasma proteins (e.g., warfarin).
- *Classic example:* trimethoprim + sulfamethoxazole block **two steps** of folate synthesis.

**Q4 → B. Synergistic.**
- Clavulanic acid blocks beta-lactamase so amoxicillin survives and kills. Clavulanic acid alone treats nothing, so the effect is greater than the sum, i.e., **synergy**.
- "Additive" would apply only if both drugs killed the bacteria separately.
- Side effects to remember: **diarrhoea; rarely cholestatic jaundice**.

**Q5 → D. Penicillins.**
- Beta-lactamase breaks the **beta-lactam ring** found in penicillins (and cephalosporins). Sulfonamides, macrolides and tetracyclines have **no beta-lactam ring**, so beta-lactamase cannot touch them.

## PART E: HOW TO ALWAYS GET THESE RIGHT

1. Draw a mental **number line**: Antagonism (<) / Additive (=) / Synergism (>).
2. The words **"greater/more than"** → synergism. **"Equal/sum"** → additive. **"Less/reduce"** → antagonism.
3. **"Beta-lactamase" ALWAYS points to penicillins/cephalosporins** (anything with a beta-lactam ring).
4. **"Clavulanic acid", "sulbactam", "tazobactam"** = inhibitors that protect beta-lactams.
5. **Agonism is never a drug-combination effect**; it is a receptor term.
6. If a question mentions **two enzymes in one pathway** (sulfamethoxazole + trimethoprim), the answer is synergy.

### Memory aids
- **"S for Super (more), A for Add (equal), A for Against (less)."**
- **Beta-lactamase breaks "-cillins and cef-s".**
- **"Calvin protects the Amoxi-boy" (clavulanic acid protects amoxicillin).**

## PART G: SECTION SUMMARY
- **Additive = sum; synergistic = more than sum; antagonistic = less than sum.**
- Reasons to combine: prevent resistance, mixed infections, empirical therapy, less toxicity, synergy.
- **Beta-lactam + aminoglycoside** is the classic synergistic pair (cell wall damage lets the aminoglycoside in).
- **Beta-lactamase breaks beta-lactam rings**; **clavulanic acid** protects amoxicillin; flucloxacillin and carbapenems resist it.
- Do **not** mix bactericidal beta-lactams with bacteriostatic drugs (antagonism).
- Complete courses and stewardship slow resistance.
