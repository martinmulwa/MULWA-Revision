# SECTION 1: Antimicrobials, Antibiotics and How They Are Classified

**Why this section matters:** Almost every antibiotic question in your past papers (mechanism, "which subunit", "which inhibits the cell wall", "which is bactericidal") is answered from this one section. Learn this well and 4 to 6 marks in every Block 1 paper become easy.

## PART A: THEORY

### A1. Definitions (slide: Antimicrobials)

| Term | Meaning in simple words | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Anti-infective / antimicrobial** | Any chemical (organic or inorganic) that kills or stops growth of a microbe (bacteria, viruses, fungi, parasites) | Antibiotics, antivirals, antifungals, antimalarials |
| **Antibiotic** | A chemical compound that kills or stops the growth of **bacteria**. Originally made by one microbe (bacteria or fungi) to kill others | Penicillin (from the fungus *Penicillium*), gentamicin, erythromycin |

> **Note-check (lecture slide):** The slide says anti-infectives treat *bacterial* infections and antibiotics are made *by bacteria or fungi*. Two small corrections to be exam-safe:
> 1. "Anti-infective/antimicrobial" is the **big umbrella** and also includes antivirals, antifungals and antiparasitics.
> 2. Many modern antibiotics are **semi-synthetic** (amoxicillin, azithromycin) or **fully synthetic** (ciprofloxacin, sulfonamides), not directly taken from a microbe. The slide on fluoroquinolones later correctly says they are synthetic.

**Rationale (why this matters):** Bacteria are living cells, so they can be attacked. Viruses are not killed by antibiotics, which is why antibiotics are **not** given for colds or flu.

### A2. Three ways to classify antibiotics (slide: Classification)

1. By **mechanism of action** (what part of the bacterium is attacked)
2. By **spectrum of activity** (how many types of bacteria are covered)
3. By **mode of action** (do they kill or only stop growth)

---

### A3. Classification by MECHANISM OF ACTION (slides: Mechanism of action + diagram)

#### Key idea: "selective toxicity"
A good antibiotic hurts the **bacterium** but not **you**. It does this by attacking things bacteria have and human cells do **not** have (or have in a different form).

| Bacterial target | Why it is a good target | Antibiotic groups (from your slides) |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Cell wall synthesis** (peptidoglycan wall) | Human cells have **no cell wall**, so these drugs are very safe. Without a wall the bacterium swells with water and bursts | **Penicillins, cephalosporins, carbapenems, monobactams** (together called **beta-lactams**), **vancomycin, bacitracin** |
| **2. Cell membrane function** | The drug damages the membrane so contents leak out | **Polymyxins** (e.g., colistin) |
| **3. Protein synthesis** (ribosomes) | Bacterial ribosomes are **70S** (made of **30S + 50S**). Human ribosomes are **80S** (40S + 60S). So drugs can block bacterial ribosomes selectively | **30S:** tetracyclines, aminoglycosides. **50S:** macrolides, clindamycin (lincosamides), linezolid, chloramphenicol, streptogramins |
| **4. Nucleic acid synthesis** (DNA/RNA) | Bacterial enzymes differ from human ones | **Quinolones** block **DNA gyrase**. **Rifampicin** blocks **RNA polymerase** |
| **5. Folic acid (folate) synthesis** | Bacteria must **make** their own folate. Humans **get folate from food**, so humans are not harmed | **Sulfonamides and trimethoprim** (together = co-trimoxazole) |

#### Reading the diagram on the slide
- **Folate pathway:** PABA → (enzyme blocked by **sulfonamides**) → DHFA → (enzyme blocked by **trimethoprim**) → THFA → DNA building blocks. Two blocks in the same pathway = **synergy** (see Section 2 and Section 11).
- **Ribosome picture:** the **50S** is the big subunit (top), the **30S** the small subunit (bottom). Arrows show which drug class sits on which one.
- **Nucleic acid box:** DNA gyrase (quinolones) and RNA polymerase (rifampicin).

> **Note-check (diagram):** The 50S list on the diagram includes **linezolid** and **streptogramins**. Linezolid is an oxazolidinone. You will rarely be tested on it, but it is correct to put it on 50S.

#### Memory aid for ribosome subunits
**"AT 30, CCEL at 50"**
- **A**minoglycosides and **T**etracyclines act at **30**S
- **C**hloramphenicol, **C**lindamycin, **E**rythromycin (all macrolides), **L**inezolid act at **50**S

#### Memory aid for the five targets: **"W-M-P-N-F"**
**W**all, **M**embrane, **P**rotein, **N**ucleic acid, **F**olate.

### A4. Classification by SPECTRUM (slide: Antimicrobial Spectrum)

- **Spectrum** = the range of microbes a drug can kill or stop.
- **Narrow-spectrum:** works on one type or a few types of bacteria. Example from slide: **isoniazid** (only mycobacteria, i.e., TB bacteria). Others: benzyl penicillin (mainly Gram-positive), vancomycin (Gram-positive only).
- **Broad-spectrum:** works on many types (Gram-positive, Gram-negative, and sometimes others). Examples from slide: **tetracycline and chloramphenicol**. Others: amoxicillin, ciprofloxacin, ceftriaxone, carbapenems.

**First-year refresher: Gram-positive vs Gram-negative**
- **Gram-positive:** thick wall, no outer membrane (e.g., *Staphylococcus*, *Streptococcus*).
- **Gram-negative:** thin wall plus an **outer membrane** that blocks many drugs (e.g., *E. coli*, *Klebsiella*, *Pseudomonas*).
- That outer membrane is why **narrow drugs like benzyl penicillin and vancomycin do not work well on Gram-negatives**.

**Rationale for nurses:** Broad-spectrum drugs also kill the normal "good" bacteria in the gut, causing **diarrhoea, thrush and C. difficile colitis**. Narrow-spectrum drugs are preferred when the germ is known.

### A5. Classification by MODE OF ACTION (slide: Mode of action)

| | **Bacteriostatic** | **Bactericidal** |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | **Stops** bacteria multiplying (the immune system must finish the job) | **Kills** bacteria directly |
| Slide examples | Tetracycline, chloramphenicol, erythromycin, lincomycin | Cephalosporin, penicillin, aminoglycosides, co-trimoxazole (and erythromycin appears again) |
| Best used when | Patient's immunity is normal | Immunity is weak (HIV, neutropenia), or in serious infections (endocarditis, meningitis, sepsis) |

> **Note-check (important, because the slides contradict themselves):**
> - **Erythromycin** appears in *both* lists. Correct answer: macrolides are **bacteriostatic** at usual doses (can be bactericidal at high levels against some germs). In an exam, treat macrolides as **bacteriostatic**.
> - **Co-trimoxazole** is on the bactericidal list here but on a later slide it is called bacteriostatic. Truth: **each part alone (sulfamethoxazole or trimethoprim) is bacteriostatic, but the two together are bactericidal** because of synergy. If an exam asks "co-trimoxazole is..." look at the options; "synergistic/bactericidal combination" is the safest.

#### Quick sorting mnemonics
- **Bacteriostatic: "ECSTaTiC"** = **E**rythromycin (macrolides), **C**lindamycin, **S**ulfonamides, **T**rimethoprim, **Ta**etracyclines, **C**hloramphenicol (also lincomycin, linezolid)
- **Bactericidal: "VCR PAM-Q"** = **V**ancomycin, **C**ephalosporins, **R**ifampicin, **P**enicillins, **A**minoglycosides, **M**etronidazole, **Q**uinolones (plus carbapenems)

**Clinical pearl:** Do **not** combine a bactericidal beta-lactam with a bacteriostatic drug (e.g., penicillin + tetracycline). Penicillin only kills **growing** bacteria. If a bacteriostatic drug stops growth, the penicillin becomes less effective (antagonism, Section 2).

## PART B: EXAM FOCUS FOR THIS SECTION

| What examiners ask | How often | Typical wording | Typical distractors |
|---|---|---|---|
| Which drugs act on **30S** or **50S** | Very high (5 papers) | "Antibiotics that inhibit protein synthesis by acting on 50S/30S subunit" | A pair containing a beta-lactam (cell wall) or a quinolone (DNA) |
| Which drugs inhibit the **cell wall** | High | "Antibiotics that inhibit cell wall formation include..." | Pairs of protein synthesis inhibitors |
| Bactericidal vs bacteriostatic | Medium | "A bactericidal drug is..." | Tetracycline, chloramphenicol, macrolides |
| Broad vs narrow spectrum | Medium | "A narrow spectrum antibiotic is..." | Amoxicillin, tetracycline |

**The question style:** Options are always **PAIRS** of drugs. **Both** drugs in the pair must belong to the class asked. One wrong drug makes the whole option wrong.

## PART D: ANSWERS AND EXPLANATIONS

**Q1 → D. Erythromycin.**
- *Why:* Erythromycin is a macrolide and sits on the **50S** subunit, blocking translocation (the ribosome cannot move along the mRNA).
- *Wrong options:* Amoxicillin (A) and ceftriaxone (C) = **cell wall** (beta-lactams). Tetracycline (B) = **30S** (the tempting one, because it also blocks protein synthesis, but on the *other* subunit).
- *Trap:* The examiner includes one drug from the **wrong subunit** to catch students who only remember "protein synthesis".

**Q2 → B. Doxycycline, gentamycin.**
- *Why:* Doxycycline (a tetracycline) blocks tRNA from attaching at the 30S site. Gentamicin (an aminoglycoside) binds 30S and makes the ribosome **misread** the code, producing faulty proteins.
- *Wrong:* A = both cell wall. C = clindamycin is 50S and ciprofloxacin is DNA gyrase. D = both 50S.

**Q3 → B. Doxycycline, gentamycin.** (Same logic as Q2.)
- *Wrong:* A = cell wall; C = 50S + DNA gyrase; D = rifampicin blocks RNA polymerase (transcription, not protein synthesis) and lincomycin is 50S.
- *Note:* "30S subunit RNA" in the stem is just clumsy wording for the 30S ribosomal subunit.

**Q4 → A. Amoxicillin, ceftriaxone.**
- *Why:* Amoxicillin (penicillin) and ceftriaxone (cephalosporin) are **beta-lactams**. They stop the cross-linking of peptidoglycan so the wall is weak and the bacterium bursts.
- *Wrong:* B = linezolid and chloramphenicol are 50S. C = doxycycline is 30S and erythromycin is 50S. D = gentamicin is 30S and lincomycin is 50S. **B, C and D are all protein synthesis inhibitors.**
- *Other cell wall drugs to know:* carbapenems, monobactams, vancomycin, bacitracin.

**Q5 → D. Erythromycin, chloramphenicol.**
- *Why:* Both bind 50S. Erythromycin blocks translocation. Chloramphenicol blocks **peptidyl transferase** (the enzyme that joins amino acids).
- *Wrong:* A = cell wall. B = both 30S. C = clindamycin is 50S (correct) but ciprofloxacin is **DNA gyrase**, so the pair fails.

### Pattern you should notice
The **same four options** (amoxicillin+ceftriaxone / doxycycline+gentamicin / clindamycin+ciprofloxacin / erythromycin+chloramphenicol) are **recycled** across 2023, 2024 and 2025 papers. Only the question stem changes.

| Option pair | Action |
|---|---|
| Amoxicillin + ceftriaxone | **Cell wall** |
| Doxycycline + gentamicin | **30S** |
| Clindamycin + ciprofloxacin | **Mixed** (50S + DNA gyrase): never the correct answer |
| Erythromycin + chloramphenicol | **50S** |

## PART E: HOW TO ALWAYS GET THESE RIGHT

1. **Read the stem for the target first** (cell wall? 30S? 50S?).
2. **Eliminate any pair containing "-cillin" or "ceph-/cef-"** unless the stem says cell wall.
3. **Put each drug in its box:** write on scrap paper "30S: A, T / 50S: C, C, E, L / Wall: beta-lactams, vanco / DNA: quinolones / RNA: rifampicin".
4. **Both drugs must fit.** A pair with one wrong drug is a decoy.
5. **"Mixed pairs" are always wrong.**
6. Watch for **spelling variants** (gentamycin/gentamicin, cetrizine/cetirizine). They are the same drug.

## PART G: SECTION SUMMARY
- Antimicrobial = umbrella; antibiotic = kills/stops **bacteria**.
- Three classifications: mechanism, spectrum, mode.
- **W-M-P-N-F** targets: Wall, Membrane, Protein, Nucleic acid, Folate.
- **30S = Aminoglycosides + Tetracyclines. 50S = Chloramphenicol, Clindamycin, Erythromycin/macrolides, Linezolid.**
- Beta-lactams + vancomycin hit the **cell wall**. Quinolones = DNA gyrase. Rifampicin = RNA polymerase. Sulfonamides/trimethoprim = folate.
- Narrow: isoniazid, benzyl penicillin, vancomycin. Broad: tetracycline, chloramphenicol, ciprofloxacin.
- Bacteriostatic **ECSTaTiC**; bactericidal **VCR PAM-Q**.
- Slides are inconsistent about erythromycin and co-trimoxazole; use the corrections above.
